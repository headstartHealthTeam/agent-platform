# Family Guidance: Judgment In Practice

Use these synthetic examples with the [Tone and Voice skill](../SKILL.md). They show how accurate
copy can still need a stronger human explanation. The supplied facts in each example are premises
for that example, not reusable clinical, insurance, or operational claims.

Choose the approach that fits the reader and format. Do not require reassurance, a quotation, a
table, a fixed number of steps, or the same sentence pattern in every article.

## Give A Useful Action Without Taking Over The Decision

Premise: the family is comparing providers and may ask about assessment and goal-setting.

Before: `You can ask your provider about the assessment and your family's priorities.`

After: `Ask your provider what the assessment involves and how your family's priorities will shape
the goals.`

The second version offers a specific question; it does not decide whether the family should choose
that provider. Keep `you can` when it genuinely communicates an option, permission, or availability.
Do not replace every modal verb with a command.

## Orient Before Explaining The Procedure

Premise: a guide helps families prepare questions after receiving an evaluation report. They do not
need to resolve every question before speaking with the evaluator.

Before: `Begin with three practical moves and start two sets of calls in parallel.`

After: `You do not need every answer before your next conversation. Start with the questions the
report raises for your family, and bring them to the evaluator.`

This recognizes uncertainty without declaring that the reader is frightened or overwhelmed. A
simple reference page may need the answer immediately instead of reassurance. Do not manufacture
emotion or a process slogan to make an opening seem warm.

## Explain First, Qualify Where It Matters

Premise: a supplied source says an assessment informs proposed goals, family input contributes, and
the recommendation differs by child. It supports no standard hours or guaranteed outcomes.

Before: `An assessment does not guarantee a particular plan. Plans may vary. You should confirm
what the provider recommends for your child.`

After: `The assessment helps the clinician propose goals for your child. Tell them which routines
are going well and where your family wants help. The recommendation is individual, so ask how the
proposed goals and hours connect to what the assessment found. No schedule guarantees an outcome.`

The qualification remains, but the paragraph gives it a purpose. Never improve confidence by
deleting a material limit or adding an unsupported benefit. A real warning may need to lead when
safety or the reader's immediate decision requires it.

## Teach The Action, Not Just Its Label

Before: `Review the proposed plan and ask questions if anything is unclear.`

After: `Ask which goals the team proposes and why they matter for your child. If a goal is unclear,
ask for an example of what working on it would look like in daily life. Share anything that does
not fit your family's priorities.`

The example does not prescribe goals or outcomes. It helps a family participate in the discussion.
For other actions, choose the explanation or example that lets the reader do the task; do not
invent facts to fill a standard list of questions.

## Make Structure Earn Its Place

Before heading: `What a transparent provider can explain`

After heading: `Questions to ask your provider`

The body should then supply those questions and briefly explain why the answers are useful. A
section comparing several team roles may work better as concise bullets or a role/responsibility
table. A nuanced explanation may work better as prose.

Keep one clear sequence when steps are useful. A substantial related topic may deserve a brief,
accurate introduction and a relevant link instead of a second checklist. Preserve required context
and material qualifications on the page where their omission would mislead. The owning content
workflow resolves scope and validates links; this skill does not invent a destination or silently
change the approved brief.
