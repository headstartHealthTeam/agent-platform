# Write Headstart Tone And Voice

Write Headstart content that is human, clear, grounded, useful, and respectful. Keep that voice
consistent while adapting the tone, detail, and energy to the audience and communication type.

This skill governs writing style only. Use the facts and constraints supplied with the task,
preserve their meaning, and return the copy the user requested.

## Voice At A Glance

Headstart sounds:

- **human, not institutional:** write like a thoughtful person speaking to another person;
- **clear, not oversimplified:** make the useful point easy to understand without talking down to
  the reader;
- **grounded, not promotional:** use honest specifics instead of hype, guarantees, or inflated
  certainty;
- **warm, not sentimental:** recognize the reader's situation without dramatizing it;
- **confident, not absolute:** state what is known directly and preserve meaningful variation;
- **professional, not corporate:** use natural language and concrete ideas instead of business
  jargon; and
- **respectful, not paternalistic:** preserve the reader's expertise, agency, and lived experience.

Warmth should come from recognition, honesty, and practical help. It should not depend on repeated
claims that Headstart cares, excessive reassurance, or emotional pressure.

## Core Writing Principles

### Start With What Matters To The Reader

Open with the question, decision, value, or next step the reader came for. Context should clarify
the point, not delay it.

### Prefer Concrete Language

Name the real action, responsibility, or benefit. Replace vague promises of `support`, `solutions`,
`tools`, and `resources` with what the reader can actually understand or do.

### Preserve Agency

Write with people, not at them. Families bring priorities and observations. Clinicians bring
expertise and judgment. RBTs evaluate whether a role fits their needs. Avoid language that frames
the organization as the hero and the audience as passive.

### Keep Roles Clear

Use the actor named in the source material. Do not casually blur Headstart, an independent
practice, a clinician, an RBT, a family, or an employer into one collective `we`. When the
distinction matters, name who provides care, makes a decision, employs someone, or supports a
process.

### Be Optimistic Without Overpromising

Headstart can be encouraging and energetic. Do not add guarantees, superlatives, universal claims,
or unsupported specifics to make the writing more compelling. Preserve material qualifiers and
variation supplied by the source.

## Adapt The Voice By Audience

### Families

Write to parents and caregivers making decisions for a child.

- Be calm, welcoming, and practical.
- Briefly acknowledge uncertainty or stress when it is relevant, then move into useful guidance.
- Put context before clinical or insurance detail.
- Explain an unfamiliar term in the sentence where it first appears.
- Help the reader understand options, expectations, and next steps without implying that one path
  fits every child or family.
- Present the child as a whole person with strengths, needs, relationships, routines, and a life
  beyond therapy.
- Preserve the family's role in asking questions, sharing priorities, and making decisions.
- Use `families`, `parents and caregivers`, or another precise label. Do not write as though the
  child is the intended reader.
- Avoid fear, blame, pressure, clinical paternalism, and deficit-heavy descriptions of autism.

Family content can recognize emotion, but the useful explanation should carry the message.

### BCBAs

Write peer-to-peer for clinicians considering ownership, leadership, partnership, or professional
resources.

- Respect clinical expertise; do not over-explain standard ABA concepts without a reason.
- Be substantive about responsibilities, decisions, practice administration, economics, and
  tradeoffs.
- Connect support to concrete work when the source provides those details.
- Keep clinical authority, professional judgment, and ownership visible.
- Use confident professional language without turning the copy into an entrepreneurship slogan.
- Avoid anti-corporate polemics, burnout exploitation, income promises, effortless-growth claims,
  and `practice of your dreams` language.

### RBTs

Write adult-to-adult for people evaluating a role, supervisor, schedule, practice, or career path.

- Be direct, encouraging, and specific.
- Prioritize details that shape fit, such as communication, supervision, mentorship, matching,
  schedule considerations, role expectations, and growth.
- Describe the employment or matching relationship exactly as provided; do not make the
  organization sound like the employer when it is not.
- Prefer `match` or `connect` to `placement` when the source describes a considered two-way fit.
- Treat becoming a BCBA as one possible goal, not the assumed path for every RBT.
- Avoid generic recruiting slogans and broad promises about hours, pay, benefits, supervision,
  advancement, culture, or every practice.

### Mixed Audiences

Name the relevant audiences and keep their interests, responsibilities, or actions distinct. Do not
flatten families and clinicians into one generic reader or shift abruptly between consumer and
professional language.

## Adapt The Voice By Communication Type

### Educational And Explanatory

- Answer the main question early.
- Organize related ideas into a sequence the reader can follow.
- Define clinical or industry terms only when the reader needs them.
- Distinguish examples from universal expectations.
- End with a useful takeaway rather than a generic slogan.

### Marketing And Promotional

- Lead with a specific, supportable value rather than a broad aspiration.
- Explain enough of the experience or mechanism to make the value credible.
- Preserve meaningful limits and variation.
- Invite action without manufacturing urgency, fear, or certainty.

### Transactional And Service

- State what happened or what is needed first.
- Keep instructions, deadlines, and next steps close to the action they affect.
- Use calm, neutral language without unrelated marketing copy.
- Translate internal system language into what the reader needs to know.

### Recruiting

- Make the opportunity and relationship understandable.
- Focus on the practical experience of the role, not empty culture claims.
- Use energy where it feels natural, but do not make every sentence a pitch.

### Editorial, Interviews, And Named-Author Stories

- Preserve the named author's or speaker's cadence, point of view, and level of candor.
- Allow more narrative and opinion than in institutional copy.
- Lightly edit for clarity without making every person sound like the same brand writer.
- Keep attributed experience distinct from general company statements.

### Social And Short-Form

- Lead with one clear idea.
- Use natural rhythm and concise sentences.
- Keep the substance; do not compress the message into generic enthusiasm.
- Use calls to action only when they add a genuine next step.

## Language And Rhythm

- Put the most important information first.
- Prefer familiar words, active voice, concrete nouns, and specific verbs.
- Aim for one main idea per sentence.
- Keep most digital paragraphs to one to three short sentences.
- Use contractions and second person when they make the copy sound natural.
- Give context before a condition, caveat, or action.
- Define an acronym at first use unless the intended audience reliably knows it.
- Use one stable term for the same concept within a communication.
- Use headings, lists, steps, or examples when they make complex material easier to follow.
- Read the draft aloud. If it sounds like a pitch deck, policy memo, implementation note, or AI
  summary, rewrite it.

Use `Headstart Health` for the organization and `Headstart` as the natural short form. Do not write
`Head Start`. Preserve standard forms such as `ABA`, `BCBA`, `RBT`, and `TRICARE`.

## Language To Avoid

Avoid generic phrases such as:

- `navigate your journey`;
- `unlock your potential`;
- `seamless experience`;
- `tailored solutions`;
- `designed to empower`;
- `robust platform`;
- `transformative care`;
- `world-class`;
- `best-in-class`; and
- `game-changing`.

Also avoid:

- vague benefits with no concrete object;
- repeated sentence openings or value propositions;
- journey metaphors when the actual step or decision can be named;
- internal system terminology when the audience only needs an action or status;
- manufactured urgency, false scarcity, or pressure to convert;
- adversarial claims about other providers, employers, or companies;
- stigmatizing or deficit-heavy descriptions of autism or support needs; and
- invented facts, stronger claims, or missing qualifiers introduced for smoother prose.

## Transformation Examples

### Family Orientation

Before: `Navigate your child's ABA journey with tailored support every step of the way.`

After: `Understand your options, what intake involves, and what to expect next.`

### BCBA Professional Copy

Before: `Escape the corporate grind and unlock the freedom to build the practice of your dreams.`

After: `Build an independent practice with control over clinical decisions and support for the
administrative work behind it.`

### RBT Recruiting Copy

Before: `Join Headstart for guaranteed hours, great mentorship, and a clear path to becoming a
BCBA.`

After: `Explore RBT roles with independent practices and learn how matching, BCBA support, and
growth opportunities work.`

### Service Copy

Before: `The intake record failed validation and was not persisted.`

After: `We could not submit your form. Check the highlighted fields, then submit it again.`

### Role Clarity

Before: `We create each child's treatment plan and track progress.`

After: `Your child's BCBA creates the treatment plan, reviews progress, and adjusts clinical goals.`

These examples demonstrate voice and style. They are not reusable factual claims.

## Style Check

Before returning the copy, confirm that:

- the intended audience is obvious;
- the opening makes the useful point clear;
- the writing sounds natural when read aloud;
- concrete language has replaced vague brand language;
- relevant people and roles remain distinct;
- the audience's agency and expertise are respected;
- supplied facts, qualifiers, and attributed meaning remain intact;
- no hype, guarantee, or unsupported specificity was added; and
- the tone fits the communication type without losing the Headstart voice.

Revise anything that sounds generic, inflated, paternalistic, overly technical, or unlike a
thoughtful person speaking clearly.
